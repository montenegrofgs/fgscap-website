Lessons Learned #07

The Cheapest Bid Can Become the Most Expensive Decision.

Lowest price does not always mean lowest cost.

In construction and development, the lowest proposal is often the easiest number to approve—and the hardest decision to defend later.

A bid can appear cheaper because it excludes scope, underestimates complexity, assumes unrealistic productivity or postpones costs that will return through change orders.

By the time the true cost becomes visible, the project may already be committed, the schedule exposed and the owner’s negotiating leverage reduced.

Disciplined procurement must compare more than price:

  • complete and clearly defined scope;
  • exclusions, assumptions and allowances;
  • contractor experience and financial capacity;
  • team quality and availability;
  • schedule credibility and procurement plan;
  • change-order history and claims behavior;
  • safety, quality and subcontractor network;
  • total cost and risk through completion.

Competence has a price. Wanting experienced people while refusing to pay fairly for their experience eventually devalues the work—and transfers risk back to the project.

This does not mean the highest bid is the best. It means the owner must understand what is actually being purchased, what remains outside the price and who is capable of delivering it.

The right decision is not the cheapest opening number. It is the strongest risk-adjusted path to completion.
