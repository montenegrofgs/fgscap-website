Lessons Learned #01

The Report Said Everything Was Fine. Then We Found a $6 Million Overrun.

Independent oversight is not overhead. It is capital protection.

Your construction report says everything is fine. But is it?

There is one question almost everyone forgets to ask during a monthly payment review: What was planned, what was actually completed, and where was the budget allocated for work that was not performed?

Now imagine that question being repeated every month across 10, 20 or more construction sites. That is where small inconsistencies become major losses.

You can have excellent contractors, suppliers, controllers and development managers. But reports and spreadsheets are not enough. Someone must independently verify what is happening on site and confirm that physical progress, payments, contracts and forecasts are aligned.

I learned this lesson personally.

At one point in my career, I was responsible for 23 simultaneous projects. During an executive committee meeting, my construction director reported that our costs had exceeded budget by approximately US$6 million.

By then, the loss had already occurred.

That experience changed the way I look at project governance. The most expensive problems are rarely created in a single month. They accumulate quietly through incomplete activities, incorrect allocations, delayed decisions, undocumented changes and forecasts that nobody challenges.

Internal executives and managers are essential. But they may sometimes hesitate to report mistakes, losses or emerging problems — especially when they are responsible for delivering the original plan.

That is why independent oversight matters.

An Owner’s Representative or Independent Engineer works from the owner’s and investor’s perspective. The role is not to replace the internal team or contractor, but to provide neutral verification, transparency and early warning.

  • Compare planned, committed and actual costs.
  • Validate payment applications against site progress.
  • Identify deviations before they become losses.
  • Challenge the cost-to-complete forecast.
  • Protect the projected return.

The greater the number of projects, the greater the need for an independent layer of control. This is not unnecessary overhead. It is capital protection.

Some lessons are learned in reports. Others are learned by losing your own money. This was one of mine.
